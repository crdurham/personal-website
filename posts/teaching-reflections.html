On Teaching Mathematics

Goals, philosophy, and practice

Published

November 4, 2026

One of my cohort members at UConn posed the following question as we were discussing our teaching statements:

What is the fundamental goal of teaching mathematics?

Seemed like a pretty simple question when I first heard it … but no response that I could come up with on the spot felt sufficient. Over time my thoughts on what the overarching goal(s) are (hopefully) be collected here, along with practices aimed at reaching them. Some entries will be from my own stream of consciousness, others will stem from student feedback, and others will follow from discussions with other math educators.

I. Goals of Teaching Mathematics

1. Give students the tools to be independent analytical thinkers and the means to use them.

I cannot claim this as my own idea, it came from the same cohort member mentioned above (email me if you need to cite them or something). The thought is this: what will they really need in the real world?

In the age of AI it is especially true that computation and rote memorization are less worthwhile than ever before; ChatGPT’s most basic model can correctly answer the majority of undergrad homework assignments and recite definitions or theorems better than I ever could … so what’s the point? There are two answers that come to mind.

  • Knowing what to ask. Coming up with the right question phrased in the right way requires fundamental understanding beyond memorization.

  • Heuristic understanding, following logical arguments. To have an intuitive understanding of what a correct answer looks like and how to get there – or, the ability to validate answers when presented with them – requires mathematical maturity and familiarity.

II. Thoughts on How to Arrive at Teaching Goals

1. Emphasize that solving a problem should feel like defending a case in court, winning an argument, or telling a story in logic.

Many students have been shown that math is about pushing numbers and symbols around on the page like stones in curling. Up to a certain point this works, but it’s not a robust methodology. Even though math machinery can be abstract nonsense, it doesn’t mean we can’t use words to explain ourselves! On the contrary, using words to justify nontrivial steps highilights the logical foundation for the readers (and writer) of the solution/proof.

2. Be Willing to Zoom Out

The meaning behind this phrase is twofold for me.

See the mathematical forest through the trees. This is what conceptual understanding is all about. We’re trying to create a mental map that guides our arguments; to build such a thing we have to make note of every branch and boulder, and we do this through many examples. It would be untenable to sift through this exhaustive logbook (pun intended) all the time, so a huge part of learning is figuring out what the important landmarks are.
Let students know you relate to them on a human level! This gets an exclamation because it’s often forgotten when we’re laser focused on the concepts we want to help them understand; life outside of mathematics can get lost when we dive down the rabbit hole. This can be effective with some audiences, wading with them into the depths of this fantasy world for an entire lecture (particularly majors or those who are acclimated to being absorbed in abstraction), but many people appreciate coming up for air now and then. Tell a story or a joke, make a song reference (my personal favorite), just something to let people know that they can relax. Of course, don’t waste a bunch of time doing this or break the rhythm of your explanation, and be authentic. An environment which lets students feel calm is more conducive to learning.

3. Don’t Undervalue Bookkeeping

Organization plays a role in problem solving that often goes unnoticed. This is true in terms of identifying hypotheses and using efficient data structures throughout an argument, but also writing in a physically organized fashion. As instructors we harp on readable answers because mathematics is about the communication of ideas (rightfully so), but writing clearly can also help the student find their way to a correct proof or numeric answer.
