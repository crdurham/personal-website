The Arc Length Functional

An Intro to Calculus of Variations

Section 128D
Dedicated to AVB
01 · Intro

Goals:

  • Given two points \(P\) and \(Q\) in space, view the problem of finding the shortest path between them as optimization of a functional.
  • Develop intuition for what a variation is through directional derivatives.
  • Show that the shortest path between two points in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) is a straight line.
  • Explore how changing the underlying geometry changes arc length minimizers, using the cylinder as an example.
Some Notation
  • \(\gamma\): lowercase Greek letter gamma. Typically represents a curve.
  • \(\varepsilon\): lowercase Greek letter epsilon. Typically represents a (small) real number.
  • \(\eta\): lowercase Greek letter eta. For us, represents a particular kind of vector field.
  • \(\frac{d}{dx}\bigg|_{x=0}f(x)\): differentiate with respect to \(x\), then evaluate at \(x=0\). E.g. \(\frac{d}{dx}\bigg|_{x=0}(x^2-2x+1)=(2x-2)\bigg|_{x=0}=-2.\)
02 · Background and Intuition

Motivation from Multivariable: The Second Derivative Test

Second Derivative Test: Suppose \(f:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}\) has continuous second partials near \((x_0,y_0)\). If \(\nabla f(x_0,y_0)=\vec{0}\), \(f_{xx}(x_0,y_0)>0\), and \(D(x_0,y_0)=\begin{vmatrix}f_{xx}(x_0,y_0)&f_{xy}(x_0,y_0)\\f_{yx}(x_0,y_0)&f_{yy}(x_0,y_0)\end{vmatrix}>0\), then \(f(x,y)\) has a local minimum at \((x_0,y_0)\).
Example

Use the Second Derivative Test to show \(f(x,y)=x^2+y^2\) has a local minimum at \((0,0)\).

Solution

First, we find critical points: \[\nabla f(x,y)=\left<2x,2y \right>=\left<0,0\right>\implies (x,y)=(0,0).\] We find \(f_{xx}(x,y)=f_{yy}(x,y)=2>0\), \(f_{xy}(x,y)=f_{yx}(x,y)=0\), and then \[D(0,0)=\begin{vmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{vmatrix}=4>0.\] By the Second Derivative Test, \(f(x,y)=x^2+y^2\) has a local minimum at \(0,0\).

03 · A Different Viewpoint

Second Derivative Test via Variation

Idea: Can we view the Second Derivative Test in a single-variable light?
Development 1: Define \(g_\vec{v}(\varepsilon)\)
Suppose \(\vec{x}=(x_0,y_0)\) is a candidate location for a minimum of \(f(x,y)\). Let \(\vec{v}=\left< a,b\right>\) be a fixed unit vector, let \(\varepsilon\) be a real number. Define \[\boxed{g_{\vec{v}}(\varepsilon) = f(\vec{x}+\varepsilon \vec{v})=f(x_0+\varepsilon a, y_0+\varepsilon b)}.\] The function \(g_{\vec{v}}(\varepsilon)\) measures how \(f\) changes along the line with direction \(\vec{v}\) that passes through \(\vec{x}\). The image of \(g_{\vec{v}}(\varepsilon)\) is the curve obtained by intersecting the surface \(z=f(x,y)\) with the vertical plane above \(\vec{x}+\varepsilon\vec{v}\).
Development 2: Differentiate \(g_\vec{v}(\varepsilon)\)
\[g_{\vec{v}}'(\varepsilon)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(\vec{x}+(\varepsilon+h) \vec{v})-f(\vec{x}+\varepsilon\vec{v})}{h}=D_{\vec{v}}f(\vec{x}+\varepsilon\vec{v})\] \[\implies \boxed{g_{\vec{v}}'(\varepsilon)=\nabla f(\vec{x}+\varepsilon\vec{v})\cdot \vec{v}}.\] Can also take the second derivative. By above, \(g_{\vec{v}}'(\varepsilon)=af_x(\vec{x}+\varepsilon \vec{v})+bf_y(\vec{x}+\varepsilon \vec{v})\), so (eventually omitting the input \(\vec{x}+\varepsilon\vec{v}\)) \[g_\vec{v}''(\varepsilon)=a(\nabla f_x(\vec{x}+\varepsilon \vec{v})\cdot \vec{v})+b(\nabla f_y(\vec{x}+\varepsilon \vec{v})\cdot \vec{v})\] \[=a(af_{xx}+bf_{xy})+b(af_{yx}+bf_{yy})=a^2f_{xx}+2abf_{xy}+b^2f_{yy}\] \[=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\end{bmatrix}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}f_{xx}&f_{xy}\\f_{yx}&f_{yy}\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{Hessian}}\begin{bmatrix}a\\b\end{bmatrix}=\vec{v}^TH\vec{v}\] \[\implies \boxed{g_{\vec{v}}''(\varepsilon)=\vec{v}^TH(\vec{x}+\varepsilon\vec{v})\vec{v}}.\]

05 · Calculus Reborn

Second Derivative Test (Reprise)

Second Derivative Test: Suppose \(f:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}\) has continuous second partials near \(\vec{x}=(x_0,y_0)\) and define \(g_{\vec{v}}(\varepsilon)=f(\vec{x}+\varepsilon \vec{v})\). If \(g_{\vec{v}}'(0)=0\) and \(g_{\vec{v}}''(0)>0\) for all unit vectors \(\vec{v}\), then \(f\) has a (strict) local minimum at \((x_0,y_0)\).
Why is this reasonable?
From Development 2, \(g_{\vec{v}}'(0)=0\) for all \(\vec{v}\) means \[\nabla f(x_0,y_0)\cdot \vec{v}=0\] for all \(\vec{v}\). It follows that \(\nabla f(x_0,y_0)=\vec{0}\), so \((x_0,y_0)\) is a critical point.

We also saw \(g_{\vec{v}}''(0)=\vec{v}^{T}H(x_0,y_0)\vec{v}\). If \(\vec{v}=\left< a,b\right>\), then positivity of this quantity reads (by completing the square) \[0 < a^2f_{xx}+2abf_{xy}+b^2f_{yy}=\mathbf{\textcolor{purple}{f_{xx}}}\left(a+\frac{f_{xy}}{f_{xx}}b\right)^2+\left(\frac{\mathbf{\textcolor{purple}{f_{xx}f_{yy}-(f_{xy})^2}}}{f_{xx}}\right)b^2.\] This relates \(g_{\vec{v}}''(0)>0\) to \(f_{xx}(x_0,y_0),\, D(x_0,y_0)>0\).
06 · Toward Arc Length Functional: Setup

Functional Fitness

Consider the points \(P=(1,0,0)\) and \(Q=(-1,0,1)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). We are tasked with finding the shortest path between \(P\) and \(Q\). Explore how to approach this as an optimization problem through the following questions.
  • Question 1: Describe the function \(L\) we want to minimize by specifying its (1) inputs, (2) outputs, and (3) equation.
    • Solution 1

    (1) \(L\) takes curves as inputs, where \(\gamma:[a,b]\to \mathbb{R}^3\), \(\gamma(a)=P\), and \(\gamma(b)=Q\). A function whose inputs are functions is called a functional.

    (2) \(L[\gamma]=\)length of \(\gamma\), meaning \(L\) outputs real numbers.

    (3) \(\displaystyle{L[\gamma]=\int_{\gamma} 1\,ds=\int_a^b|\gamma'(t)|\,dt}\). This is the arc length functional.

  • Question 2: How would you (roughly) take the directional derivative of \(L[\gamma]\)? (Hint: Recall \(D_{\vec{v}}f(x_0,y_0)=g_{\vec{v}}'(0)\).)
    • Solution 2

    Let's keep with the idea we've been using and look at perturbations. Here our inputs are curves \(\gamma:[a,b]\to \mathbb{R}^3\) which connect \(P\) to \(Q\); symbolically, we can deform to \[\gamma\mapsto \gamma+\varepsilon\eta\] where \(\varepsilon\) is a real number still, but \(\eta\) requires some thought or explanation. For now, just note \(\eta\) plays the role of the direction vector \(\vec{v}\) from earlier slides. Then, define the single-variable function \[G_{\eta}(\varepsilon)=L[\gamma+\varepsilon\eta].\]

    Finally, we can compute ordinary derivatives! \(G'_{\eta}(0)=\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}L[\gamma+\varepsilon\eta],\;\;G''_{\eta}(0)=\frac{d^2}{d\varepsilon^2}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}L[\gamma+\varepsilon\eta]\).

07 · Toward First Example: Variational Vector Field Visual

What's Up with \(\eta\)?

\(\eta:[a,b]\to \mathbb{R}^3\) is a smooth vector field along \(\gamma\) which satisfies \(\eta(a)=\eta(b)=\vec{0}\) so that the endpoints of \(\gamma\) are fixed: \(\gamma(a)+\varepsilon\eta(a)=P\) and \(\gamma(b)+\varepsilon\eta(b)=Q\).

08 · Toward First Example: Summary

Feeling Perturbed

Calculus Version: Fix \(\vec{x}=(x_0,y_0)\). Define \(g_{\vec{v}}(\varepsilon)=f(\vec{x}+\varepsilon \vec{v})\). Then \[ \boxed{g_{\vec{v}}'(0)= \left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}f(\vec{x}+\varepsilon \vec{v}) =\nabla f(\vec{x})\cdot \vec{v}} \] and \[ \boxed{g_{\vec{v}}''(0)=\vec{v}^{T}H(\vec{x})\vec{v}} \] give information relating to whether \(f\) has an extremum at \(\vec{x}\).

Variational Version: Fix a curve \(\gamma:[a,b]\to\mathbb R^3\) connecting \(P\) to \(Q\). Let \(\eta:[a,b]\to\mathbb R^3\) satisfy \(\eta(a)=\eta(b)=\vec 0\). Define \[ G_{\eta}(\varepsilon)=L[\gamma+\varepsilon\eta]. \] Then \[ \boxed{G_{\eta}'(0)= \left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0} L[\gamma+\varepsilon\eta]}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\boxed{G_{\eta}''(0)=\frac{d^2}{d\varepsilon^2}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}L[\gamma+\varepsilon\eta]} \]

give information relating to whether \(L\) has an extremum at \(\gamma\). These are called the first variation and second variation respectively. In particular: \(G_\eta'(0)=0\) and \(G_\eta''(0)>0\) for all nonzero \(\eta\) implies (roughly, ignoring some technical details) \(L[\gamma]\) is a local minimum of the functional \(L\).
09 · Technical Simplifications

A Convenient Alternative: Energy Functional

  • Given \(\gamma_0:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^3\) with \(\gamma_0(0)=P\), \(\gamma_0(1)=Q\), and \(|\gamma_0'(t)|>0\) for all \(t\), we can find \[\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^3,\;\;|\gamma'(t)|=C,\] with the same image as \(\gamma_0\). We can always reparametrize to have constant speed.
  • If we define the energy functional on all sufficiently smooth fixed-endpoint curves \(\gamma:[0,1]\to \mathbb{R}^3\) by \[E[\gamma]=\frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 |\gamma'(t)|^2\,dt,\] then minimizing energy gives us the same geometric paths as minimizing length: energy-minimizing curves have constant speed, and their images are length-minimizing.
Why use \(E\)?
It's easier to differentiate \[ |\gamma'(t)+\varepsilon\eta'(t)|^2 \] than \[ |\gamma'(t)+\varepsilon\eta'(t)| =\sqrt{(\gamma'(t)+\varepsilon\eta'(t)) \cdot(\gamma'(t)+\varepsilon\eta'(t))}. \] No square root!

That, and constant-speed parametrizations are nice too.

Final Technical Fact:
For \(F(\varepsilon,t)\) sufficiently smooth, we can differentiate under the integral sign:

\[\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\left(\int_0^1 F(\varepsilon, t)\,dt\right)=\int_0^1\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}F(\varepsilon,t)\right)dt.\]

10 · Cut Straight to the Point

First Variation of Energy in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

We are finally in a position to compute the first variation of a functional. Despite all the scary looking terminology and notation, it's ultimately the kind of derivative we took in Math 1131.

Calculation of \(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}E[\gamma+\varepsilon\eta]\)
Throughout, \(\gamma(t)=\langle x(t),y(t),z(t)\rangle\); there are no restrictions on the component functions a priori because the path can go wherever in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Note \(E[\gamma+\varepsilon\eta]=\frac{1}{2}\int_0^1|(\gamma+\varepsilon\eta)'|^2\,dt=\frac{1}{2}\int_0^1|\gamma'+\varepsilon\eta'|^2\,dt\). Expanding the integrand, \[|\gamma'+\varepsilon\eta'|^2=(\gamma'+\varepsilon\eta')\cdot(\gamma'+\varepsilon\eta')=|\gamma'|^2+2\varepsilon(\gamma'\cdot\eta')+\varepsilon^2|\eta'|^2\implies \textcolor{purple}{\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}(|\gamma'+\varepsilon\eta'|^2)}=\textcolor{purple}{2(\gamma'\cdot\eta')+2\varepsilon|\eta'|^2}.\] This shows \[\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}E[\gamma+\varepsilon\eta]=\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_0^1|\gamma'+\varepsilon\eta'|^2\,dt\right)\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}=\frac{1}{2}\int_0^1\textcolor{purple}{\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}(|\gamma'+\varepsilon\eta'|^2)}\,dt\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}=\frac{1}{2}\int_0^1(\textcolor{purple}{2(\gamma'\cdot\eta')+2\varepsilon|\eta'|^2})\,dt\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0},\] so \[\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}E[\gamma+\varepsilon\eta]=\int_0^1 \gamma'\cdot \eta'\,dt.\] We have more information about \(\eta\) than \(\eta'\)...use integration by parts: \[\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}E[\gamma+\varepsilon\eta]=\int_0^1 \gamma'\cdot \eta'\,dt=\underbrace{\gamma'(t)\eta(t)\bigg|_0^1}_{\textcolor{blue}{=0}}-\int_0^1\gamma''\cdot \eta\,dt\] \[\therefore\;\;\boxed{\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}E[\gamma+\varepsilon\eta]=-\int_0^1\gamma''\cdot \eta\,dt}.\] What does this say about critical points of the energy functional?
11 · Candidates for Energy Minimizers

What Does \(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}E[\gamma+\varepsilon\eta]\) Tell Us?

Question: If \(\gamma\) is a critical point of \(E\), i.e. \(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}E[\gamma+\varepsilon\eta]=-\int_0^1\gamma''\cdot \eta\,dt=0\) for all admissible \(\eta\) which are not identically \(\vec{0}\), what can we say about \(\gamma\)?
Solution
It must be that \(\gamma\) satisfies the ordinary differential equation \[\boxed{\gamma''(t)=\vec{0}}.\]
How to justify \(\gamma''(t)=0\)?
Suppose \(|\gamma''(t)|>0\) for \(0\leq t_1< t < t_2\leq 1\); then we can construct \(\eta\) such that \(\gamma''(t)\cdot \eta(t) > 0 \) for \(t_1< t < t_2\) and \(\eta(t)=\vec{0}\) otherwise. But then, \[\int_0^1\gamma''\cdot \eta\,dt=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\gamma''\cdot \eta\,dt >0,\] which is a contradiction.

This means in terms of components that \(\gamma(t)=\left< x(t),y(t)\right>\) satisfies \[\left< x''(t),y''(t),z''(t)\right>=\left< 0,0,0 \right>.\] Then, taking repeated antiderivatives... \[x''(t)=0\implies x'(t)=A_1\implies x(t)=A_1t+A_2,\] \[y''(t)=0\implies y'(t)=B_1\implies y(t)=B_1t+B_2.\] \[z''(t)=0\implies z'(t)=C_1\implies z(t)=C_1t+C_2.\] That is, \(\gamma(t)=\left< A_1t+A_2, B_1t+B_2, C_1t+C_2\right>\) is a straight line! We also have the boundary conditions \(\gamma(0)=P=(1,0,0),\;\gamma(1)=Q=(-1,0,1)\), which imply \[\boxed{\gamma(t)=\left< -2t+1,0,t \right>,\;\;0\leq t\leq 1}.\]
12 · Checking Second Derivative

Second Variation of Energy Functional

Question: What is the second variation of the energy functional, \(\frac{d^2}{d\varepsilon^2}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}E[\gamma+\varepsilon\eta]\)? What does this say about the nature of the critical point (straight line) we just found?
Solution
Recall we calculated \[\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}E[\gamma+\varepsilon\eta]= \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1({2(\gamma'\cdot\eta')+2\varepsilon|\eta'|^2})\,dt;\] taking the second derivative and evaluating at \(\varepsilon=0\) yields \[\boxed{\frac{d^2}{d\varepsilon^2}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}E[\gamma+\varepsilon\eta]=\int_0^1|\eta'|^2\,dt}.\] This shows that the second variation is positive for all nontrivial perturbations (\(\eta\not\equiv \vec{0}\)), so \(E\) is 'concave up' everywhere. In fancier terminology, it's strictly convex.

In particular, this means the straight line \(\gamma\) obtained previously is the unique minimizer of \(E\), hence gives the minimum value of \(L\).

13 · A Whole New World

Change of Scenery: Cylinder

View \(P\) and \(Q\) as points on the cylinder \[\{(x,y,z):x^2+y^2=1\}\] and restrict possible paths to this surface. How does the setup of the problem change?
What do paths \(\gamma\) look like?
It's no longer the case that \(\gamma(t)=\langle x(t),y(t),z(t)\rangle\) with no restrictions. To stay on the cylinder, we need \(x(t)^2+y(t)^2=1\). Using standard cylindrical coordinates, we write \[ \boxed{\gamma(t)= \langle\cos(\theta(t)),\sin(\theta(t)),z(t)\rangle.} \] Here, \(\theta(t)\) is the angular coordinate of our position, while \(z(t)\) describes our height.
How do admissible perturbations \(\eta\) look?
We can only consider perturbations \(\gamma+\varepsilon\eta\) that remain on the cylinder. We can move vertically (in the \(z\)-direction) or angularly (in the \(\theta\)-direction), so we perturb the functions \(\theta(t)\) and \(z(t)\): \[ \boxed{\theta_\varepsilon(t)=\theta(t)+\varepsilon u(t)}, \qquad \boxed{z_\varepsilon(t)=z(t)+\varepsilon v(t)}. \] This gives a new curve \[ \boxed{\gamma_\varepsilon(t)= \left\langle \cos(\theta+\varepsilon u), \sin(\theta+\varepsilon u), z+\varepsilon v \right\rangle}, \] which stays on the cylinder since \[(\cos(\theta+\varepsilon u))^2+(\sin(\theta+\varepsilon u))^2=1.\] As before, we require the endpoints to remain fixed, so \(u(0)=u(1)=v(0)=v(1)=0\).
What happens to the energy?
Differentiating our parametrization gives, by the chain rule, \[ \gamma_\varepsilon'(t)= \left\langle -\sin(\theta_\varepsilon(t))\theta_\varepsilon'(t), \cos(\theta_\varepsilon(t))\theta_\varepsilon'(t), z_\varepsilon'(t) \right\rangle. \] Consequently, \[ |\gamma_\varepsilon'(t)|^2 =(\theta_\varepsilon'(t))^2+(z_\varepsilon'(t))^2. \] So the energy becomes \[ \boxed{ E[\gamma_\varepsilon]=\frac12\int_0^1 \left(\theta_{\varepsilon}'(t)^2+z_{\varepsilon}'(t)^2\right)\,dt. } \]
14 · Shortest Paths Depend on Geometry

What is the shortest path from \(P\) to \(Q\) on the cylinder?

First Variation
From \(\theta_\varepsilon=\theta+\varepsilon u\), \(z_\varepsilon=z+\varepsilon v\), \[E[\gamma_\varepsilon]=\frac12\int_0^1 \left((\theta'+\varepsilon u')^2+(z'+\varepsilon v')^2\right)\,dt\] \[\implies \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}E[\gamma_\varepsilon]=\int_0^1((\theta'+\varepsilon u')u'+(z'+\varepsilon v')v')\,dt.\] Evaluating at \(\varepsilon=0\), we find \[\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}E[\gamma_\varepsilon]=\int_0^1(\theta' u'+z'v')\,dt.\] We can use integration by parts as before to show that \[\boxed{\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}E[\gamma_\varepsilon]=-\int_0^1(\theta''u+z''v)\,dt}.\]
Second Variation
From the calculation above, \[ \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}E[\gamma_\varepsilon]=\int_0^1((\theta'+\varepsilon u')u'+(z'+\varepsilon v')v')\,dt;\] differentiating again, \[\frac{d^2}{d\varepsilon^2}E[\gamma_\varepsilon]=\int_0^1((u')^2+(v')^2)\,dt.\] This is independent of \(\varepsilon\) much like before, so \[\boxed{\frac{d^2}{d\varepsilon^2}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}E[\gamma_\varepsilon]=\int_0^1((u')^2+(v')^2)\,dt}.\] Once again: the second variation is positive for any perturbation which is not identically 0.
Conclusion
At a critical curve, the first variation must vanish for every choice of \((u,v)\). As a result, \[-\int_0^1(\theta''u+z''v)\,dt=0\implies \theta''=0,\;\;z''=0.\] This means the shortest path must be a line...in the \((\theta,z)\)-plane: \[\theta(t)=A_1t+A_2,\;\;z(t)=B_1t+B_2.\] The cylinder takes the \((\theta,z)\)-plane and wraps it up in the \(\theta\)-direction, so we have \[\gamma(t)=\langle \cos(A_1t+A_2), \sin(A_1t+A_2), B_1t+B_2 \rangle.\] By the boundary conditions \(\gamma(0)=(1,0,0)\) and \(\gamma(1)=(-1,0,1),\) we can show \[\boxed{\gamma_n(t)=\langle \cos((2n+1)\pi t), \sin((2n+1)\pi t), t \rangle,\;\;n\in \mathbb{Z}}\] are all critical! Moreover, since the second variation is positive for all nontrivial perturbations of any curve \(\gamma\), each of the above is a local minimum of \(E\), hence a local minimum of \(L\)!

...but which one (ones) is (are) the global minimum (minima)?

15 · Helices
Finding global minimizers
\[L[\gamma_n]=\int_0^1\sqrt{(2n+1)^2\pi^2+1}\,dt=\sqrt{(2n+1)^2\pi^2+1}\] This is smallest for \(n=0\) and \(n=-1\), corresponding to the pieces of helices that traverse in opposite directions: \[\boxed{\gamma_0(t)=\langle \cos(\pi t), \sin(\pi t), t \rangle},\;\;0\leq t\leq 1\] \[\boxed{\gamma_{-1}(t)=\langle \cos(\pi t), -\sin(\pi t), t \rangle},\;\;0\leq t\leq 1.\]
